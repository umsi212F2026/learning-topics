A small-animal veterinary clinic checks on every pet two days after its visit, and calls the owners
of any pet that isn't doing well. About 150 owners reply in a typical week.

1. Text each owner two days after their pet's visit, asking how the pet is doing. (a program)
2. Read each owner's reply and mark it "doing well" or "needs a call", with a one-line reason.
   (who?)
3. Go over the marked replies, change any mark that is wrong, and call each owner marked "needs a
   call". (the veterinary technician)
4. For the few pets the calls show are not recovering, decide whether the pet comes back in,
   changes medicine or waits a few more days. (who?)
5. Text each of those owners what was decided, with an appointment time if there is one. (a
   program)

In the clinic manager's words: "Owners type their replies however they like, from a single
thumbs-up to three paragraphs, and plenty of households answer about two pets in one message, so
it isn't always clear which pet a symptom belongs to. Owners often mention their children by name,
as in 'Maya says Biscuit is eating again.' Our technician reads every marked reply before she
starts her calls, and a wrong mark rarely gets past her. The clinic has used the same texting
service since it opened in 2015. Only four or five pets a week turn out not to be recovering, and
each is different: a dog that eats but still limps, a cat whose owner can't get the pills into
her. The technician's notes on those pets usually record what the owner told her about money, such
as being on a payment plan or not being able to afford another visit. If we tell an owner to wait
and the pet needed to be seen, the next stop is the emergency hospital, which can cost that family
well over a thousand dollars, and nobody looks at the decision again once the text goes out. Dr.
Okafor decides these between surgeries, usually not until late afternoon. The recheck room was
repainted last spring."
