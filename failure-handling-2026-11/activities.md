# Activities: failure handling

Candidate activities for the study phase. More than will be used; the tutor chooses among them with the learner.

The initial study comes to about 35 minutes, inside the 40 the lesson plan allows: about 12 for `a-read-failure-handling` (the reading is about 1,450 words, about 7 minutes on its own), about 3 a word for the three words, so 9, and meeting both capabilities. That takes seven questions, one per case: four for `c-judge-rerunnable` and three for `c-judge-detection`. Through the two quick checks, `a-pick-rerun-outcome` and `a-pick-noticed-or-not`, a question takes about 2 minutes, so about 14. Through `a-critique-a-workflow`, one scenario's six questions carry six of the seven cases in about 12 minutes, and the tutor sets one quick-check question for the case left over.

Every question is multiple choice, asks one thing, names one goal and carries one case. A rerun question always offers all four cases of `c-judge-rerunnable` as its options, and a detection question always offers all three cases of `c-judge-detection`, each in a fixed order, worded as the generators below give them. No question asks for a reason, for a fix, or for what to tell an agent. No question's text gives away another's answer. Learner-facing text states the task, never the scoring.

In this topic's banks and live questions, every workflow is a made-up one described in words, running on one person's laptop, with at least one step done by an agent following a skill file the person wrote. Never the learner's own app, repo, machine, project or Problem Set 5 workflow; never an internship or job postings tracker; never the reading's grading example (its TA, drafts, grades sheet or posting program) or any other homework or grading workflow. Every scenario states every fact its answer needs: what each step does with each item, any test a step uses to pass over items already done, where a run stopped, what has happened to the results since, every check and review in the workflow and exactly what each one looks at, and where a step reports trouble and whether anyone looks there. No scenario contains code, a query, SQL or a command; a kept thing is called by what it holds ("the listings list", "a notes file for each listing"), with no file format, database or storage product named. No real product, site or brand is named; a source is "a rental site", "a newsletter she subscribes to". The topic's words idempotent and silent failure appear in the fixed option wording, with no gloss; scenarios never use them, or fail loudly, since in a scenario each would point at an answer.

**No matching words to a case.** A scenario never uses an option's own words or the goal's example phrases: in a rerun scenario, not "twice", "second", "again", "duplicate", "half", "partly", "incomplete", "repeat", "used", "checked" or "different"; in a detection scenario, not "silent", "notice", "hear", "looks right", "catch" or "log". An action outside the workflow is never sending a message, an email or a text, and never one with an obvious guard against repeats (a library hold, a sign-up that refuses a second try); it is putting in an order, posting to a board other people read, or adding an event to a calendar other people share. It always reaches someone else, so it can't be read as an entry in something the workflow keeps: never an event on the person's own calendar or a note in their own file. A mistake that looks like a right result is never called a misread; it is said as what came out ("took the move-in date as the rent due date", "rated the listing 'skip' though it meets every one of her needs"). The facts that point at one case in one scenario point at another elsewhere, as each generator's spread requires: a stop in the middle of writing appears in scenarios keyed safe and keyed replaced as well as half-written; a person who has already looked over results appears in scenarios keyed safe as well as replaced; a check or a review appears in scenarios keyed not noticed as well as noticed, because it looks at something other than the mistake; and a file of problems appears in a scenario keyed noticed, because the person reads it.

## Check notes

2026-11-13. Every check in this file supplies the stop or the mistake and states every fact the answer needs, and every check is a pick from fixed options. Meeting both capabilities shows the learner can judge a step once it is fully described. It does not show they can find those facts in a workflow of their own, or decide which stops and mistakes are worth worrying about. Those are what the Problem Set 5 critique and the Nov 17 class ask for.

## Goals

| id                   | Goal | Criterion: what gets examined, and what counts |
| -------------------- | ---- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| `o-orientation`      | get the shape of this area before working on any particular part of it | `orientation` |
| `c-judge-rerunnable` | judge whether a step is safe to run again after a run stops partway | given a described step and a run that stopped partway through it, says whether running the step again from the start would be safe, and if not, which of these would go wrong: items done a second time, a store left half-written, or a different outcome from one that a later step or a person has already used or checked. Cases: `duplicates`, `half-written`, `replaces-used`, `already-safe` |
| `c-judge-detection`  | judge whether a mistake made while running a step would be noticed | given a described workflow, one of its steps, and a mistake that step could make, says whether the mistake would be noticed, and if not, which of these is why: the step can tell something went wrong but says so only somewhere nobody will look, or not at all; or the step produces a wrong result that looks like a right one and no check in the workflow would catch it. Cases: `unheard`, `looks-right`, `detected` |

## Activities

### `a-read-failure-handling`

- **serves:** `all`
- **supports:** orient
- **checks:** `o-orientation`
- **artifact:** a reading the course wrote for this topic, `tasks/read-failure-handling.md` in this folder, "When a step goes wrong" (about 1,450 words, about 7 minutes). No external reading is used: the instructor wanted a course-written one. It continues the made-up example of the last three topics' readings, a teaching assistant who grades about 200 homework submissions a week. It is a draft the instructor has not yet reviewed, so this entry relies only on its section names and the words it bolds. Sections: an untitled opening (two judgments to make about each step, and the TA's workflow); Incomplete runs and incorrect results; When a run stops partway; Would anyone notice?; Failing loudly; Telling your agent. Words in place, each in bold at its definition: idempotent, silent failure, fails loudly (the word's form here is fail loudly; "failing loudly" appears earlier, unbolded, in the opening and in a heading). Also in bold, though none is a word of this topic: the labels of the three ways a rerun goes wrong (items done a second time, a store left half-written, a different outcome replacing one already used) and of the two reasons a mistake goes unnoticed (the step could tell, but nobody hears; the result looks right, and nothing checks it). Once the instructor has reviewed the reading, confirm that the stop below still falls just before the page gives its own answer.
- **verified:** 2026-11-13
- **learner does:** reads the page once, top to bottom, and stops once, in When a run stops partway, at the end of its opening paragraph and before its bulleted list, answering before reading on ("I don't know yet" is an honest answer): if the TA runs the stopped step again from the start, what could go wrong? The bulleted list gives the page's own answer, which the learner then reads. Then the close, about 2 minutes, with the reading still beside them: answers the one question the tutor puts: with this page beside you, could you now take a short description of a workflow step, say whether it would be safe to run again after a run stopped partway through it, and say whether a mistake it made would be noticed?
- **tutor role:** explainer
- **tutor does:** puts the reading in front of the learner by opening the file `tasks/read-failure-handling.md` from the topic folder on the local filesystem, so the learner's agent shows it formatted in a sidebar; does not paste it into the chat. Stays quiet through the reading except at the stop and when asked. At the stop, takes the learner's answer first and replies with one near-miss question rather than a verdict, then lets the learner read on to the page's own answer. What the tutor looks for is something specific to the stopped step that a rerun could spoil: submissions done over, an unfinished result, or results someone has already acted on changing. The answer need not match the list. An answer of "nothing, just run it again" gets one near-miss question ("what happens to the submissions it had already finished?"). Does not set or rehearse any word question from `a-words`: does not ask the learner to define any of the three words, to say what separates a word from its nearest confusable in goals.md (idempotent and deterministic, fail loudly and fail fast), or to correct a misused sentence. Does not set or rehearse a question from this topic's capability activities for any workflow beyond the one the reading works through; if the learner asks how to do that, says that is what `a-pick-rerun-outcome`, `a-pick-noticed-or-not` and `a-critique-a-workflow` are for. Before the learner starts reading, not during it, says briefly three things: that fixing what they find is their agent's work, so the Telling your agent table is there for Problem Set 5 and the Nov 17 class, and no question turns on it; that no question asks how to fix a step, only what would go wrong and whether a mistake would be noticed; and that the difference between failing loudly and failing fast is a word question, never a capability one. At the close, puts the readiness question as written above and rules on the answer.
- **done when:** criterion met. The bar for this goal is did it once and help is expected throughout, so the ruling is on the learner's answer to the readiness question, not on the stop or on whether the tutor thinks they are ready. A plain yes is `criterion: met`. A hedge, with no plain no, is `criterion: unclear`: ask which judgment feels shaky, go over its section once more (When a run stops partway, or Would anyone notice?), and put the question again. A second hedge stays `unclear`: the tutor offers one of the capability activities with help as the next step. A plain no is `criterion: not met`: record it, ask what is missing, and offer to go back over the section that bears on it; don't put the question again in the same sitting. This goal isn't required, so a no never blocks anything else the learner wants to try.
- **worked example:** n/a
- **doesn't show:** an indication of readiness is all this goal asks for and all this shows. It shows nothing about the three words, which have their own supply in `a-words`, and nothing about either capability: the reading works one example, the stop is helped, ungraded and answered just before the page gives its own answer, and the readiness question asks only whether the learner thinks they could do the same. The reading is one course-written page built on one grading workflow, so it shows nothing of a workflow in another domain, such as the personal trackers the capability activities use.
- **offer as:** this topic's orientation: one short page the course wrote, following the same grading workflow as the last topics' readings, from the two places a failure can be, through what a rerun can spoil and why a mistake goes unnoticed, to failing loudly. One short stop and a one-question close. About 12 minutes.
- **check note:** The stop sits right after the sentence "Here are three ways it can go wrong", so the learner already knows there are three. An answer that names only one of them is still a good answer at the stop. Take it and move on.

### `a-pick-rerun-outcome`

- **serves:** `c-judge-rerunnable`
- **supports:** attempt
- **checks:** `c-judge-rerunnable`
- **artifact:** no external source. A described step of a personal workflow and a run that stopped partway through it, told in an opening sentence, a bullet per step and a closing paragraph, with one five-choice question, from this activity's bank or written live per the generator below. About 2 minutes a question.
- **learner does:** reads the scenario, then picks which of five outcomes running the step again from the start would have: nothing goes wrong, or one of the four things that can.
- **tutor role:** examiner
- **tutor does:** sets the question as served, without rewording it or hinting; never names the cases, which fact matters, or which option the key expects, and never says what is wrong with an option before the learner has chosen. Gives help whenever it is asked for, and records the attempt as helped. When the generator is run live, writes the key and the case into the record before showing the question. Labels the attempt with the question's path, or `a-pick-rerun-outcome/<shape>` when run live, and records the case with `--cases`. Rules from the rubric's `answer`. If the learner asks how the step should be fixed, says that is the agent's to choose once the learner can say what worries them, and this question doesn't ask it.
- **done when:** the criterion met with no help, case by case. Each question gets one ruling: the sound option passes the case the question carries, and the others pass none. There is no half credit.
- **generator:** **A scenario** is one made-up personal workflow on one person's laptop, five to eight sentences in all, in the third person about that person, who is named. Every scenario in a bank names a different person, and none uses a name from this file's illustrations. One step is "the step" below, which the scenario names with a short handle ("the assessing agent", "the ordering program"), as it names every step.

  **Layout.** An opening sentence says who the person is and what they want. Then comes one bullet per step, in the order the steps run, each saying who does that step and what it does; a check, or a later step such as a shortlist program, gets its own bullet, and the person's routine goes in the bullet for what they read, or in its own. Then a paragraph says what happened: where the run stopped, and what has happened to the results since. Sentences are short, one fact each. `tasks/a-pick-rerun-outcome/concert-ticket-resale.md` shows the shape.

  **Kept things told apart.** When a scenario has both a list's entries and saved pages or files, it says plainly what each is, and never joins a fact about one to a fact about the other in one sentence. When a step does two things to each item, it says so, then names each ("For each new listing, it does two things: it saves a copy of the listing's web page in the listings folder, and it adds an entry for the listing to the listings list.").

  **No test for done unless stated.** A step does only what the scenario says it does. A step with no test for done is described without one, and no sentence says it doesn't check: never "passing over none", "pays no attention to what the list holds already", "doesn't look at what is already there", or "even if the list already has one". A rubric settles such a question on the step's description and the absence of any test, on the same footing as unread by default in `a-pick-noticed-or-not`.

  Every sentence about a step's work says who does it ("the program adds", "the agent passes over"), never "the step adds" or "a step goes through"; "the step" is only the name of the stage, as in the question. At least half of a bank's scenarios are a small personal tracker with acquire, assess and extract steps (a program collects items from somewhere, an agent judges each one, a program or an agent pulls out fields), and the step is one of those three; the rest are other personal workflows (a household's receipts, a club's sign-ups). The three are kept separate, as Problem Set 5 lays them out: the assessing agent judges each item (a rating, a verdict, notes on how well it fits), and pulling fields out of an item (a deadline, a price, an award amount) is always the extract step's work, never folded into the assess step. In the bullets and the closing paragraph, never under labels, are these facts:
  - what the step does with each item, in plain words: adds a new entry for it to a kept thing ("adds an entry for each new listing to the listings list"); writes the item's own result in place, overwriting whatever is there ("writes each listing's rating into its row, overwriting any rating already there"; "writes a notes file for each listing"); or does something outside the workflow for it ("puts in an order for each seed packet", "adds an event to the family's shared calendar for each game"), always one with no stated guard against repeats (never a library hold or a sign-up that refuses a second try);
  - who does the step: an agent following the person's skill file, or a program. Where a program writes results in place, what it works from, and that those inputs stay as they were ("the saved pages are never changed after they are saved");
  - if the step passes over items already done, its test for done, said plainly and never judged ("it passes over any listing that already has a notes file"; "it passes over any listing on its finished list, which it saves when a run ends"; "it passes over any listing marked 'rated', a mark it adds only after the whole rating is written"); a step with no test for done is described without one, as above;
  - in the closing paragraph, where the run stopped, and why, in one sentence: between two items ("the laptop's battery ran out just after it finished the 40th of 70 listings") or in the middle of writing one item's result ("while it was writing the notes for the 41st");
  - in the closing paragraph, what has happened to the results so far since the stop: nothing ("nobody has opened the list since"), or a person or a later step has acted on some of them ("Lena has gone through the first 25 ratings and booked viewings for three of them"; "at noon the digest program put the first 30 ratings into the week's shortlist").

  **Exactly one outcome.** Every scenario is built so that exactly one option is right, and states the fact that rules out each other option, as the key gives it; where that fact is that the step has no test for done, the step's description, which has none, is that fact. When a run stops in the middle of writing one item's result and a person or a later step has acted on results since, the scenario says the one acting used only finished results ("all of them among the first 30"), so the partly written result can't make option 4 right as well. A step that adds entries never stops in the middle of writing one (or it would leave a partial entry as well as adding more), and nobody has acted on its results. A step that writes results in place never adds entries and does nothing outside the workflow. A step that acts outside the workflow writes nothing a later step or person relies on, and stops between items.

  **The question** reads: "If <person> runs <the step> again from the start, which of these happens?", then five numbered options, always in this order and these words:
  1. "Nothing goes wrong: as it stands, the step is idempotent."
  2. "Duplicate records get added."
  3. "An action outside the workflow gets repeated."
  4. "A partly written result gets trusted or tripped over."
  5. "A different result replaces one that a person or a later step has already used or checked."

  `type: mcq`. The question carries the one case its answer exercises: answer 1 `already-safe`, answer 2 `duplicates` (shape `adds-again`), answer 3 `duplicates` (shape `acts-again`), answer 4 `half-written`, answer 5 `replaces-used`.

  **Shapes**, each with the case it carries. Between them every case is carried.
  - `adds-again` (`duplicates`): the step adds a new entry for each item and either is described with no test for done or has one the stopped run fools because the record it tests is written only when a run ends ("it passes over any listing on its finished list, which it saves when a run ends"). The run stopped between items. Nobody has acted on the results. Key: the items before the stop get a duplicate entry; no outside action, since the step only adds entries; not half-written, since the stop came between items; not replaced, since adding leaves the first entries as they were and nobody has acted on them.
  - `acts-again` (`duplicates`): the step does something outside the workflow for each item that reaches someone else (puts in an order, posts to a board other people read, adds an event to a calendar other people share), with no test for done or one the stopped run fools as above. The run stopped between items. Key: the items before the stop get the outside action repeated, which no later run can take back; no duplicate records, since the step adds nothing to any kept thing; not half-written, since the stop came between items; not replaced, since nothing in the workflow is rewritten.
  - `trusts-partial` (`half-written`): the step writes each item's own result (a notes file, a field in the item's entry) and passes over any item that already has one, by a test the partial result passes ("already has a notes file"; "whose summary field isn't empty"). The run stopped in the middle of writing one item's result. Nobody has acted on the results. Key: the item the run stopped on has only part of its result, and the rerun passes over it; no duplicates, since the finished items are passed over and each item has one result; not replaced, since finished items are passed over and nobody has acted on them.
  - `redoes-acted-on` (`replaces-used`): an agent writes each item's result in place, overwriting any result already there, and is described with no test for done. Since the stop, a person or a later step has acted on some of the results. The run stopped between items or in the middle of writing one; when it stopped in the middle of writing one, the scenario says the one acting used only finished results ("at noon the digest program put four listings into the week's shortlist, all of them among the first 25"). Key: the agent may judge an item differently when asked a second time, and its new result overwrites one the person or later step has already acted on, so they no longer agree; no duplicates, since each item keeps one result and nothing outside the workflow happens; not half-written, since the rerun rewrites every item, the unfinished one included, and the one acting used only finished results.
  - `already-safe` (`already-safe`): two variants, which the key names. In both the step is idempotent, which option 1 says, so an agent that rewrites results with no test for done is never keyed here, even when nobody has acted on its results. `sound-skip`: the step passes over items already done, and its test is one the stopped run cannot fool (a mark added only after the whole result is written); the scenario says the agent or program doing the step writes each item's result, overwriting whatever is there ("the agent writes each listing's notes file, overwriting any notes already in it"), so the item it stopped on, which has no mark, gets its result written whole from the start. `same-inputs`: a program writes each item's result in place from saved inputs that never change, so it writes the same result each time; the person has already looked over some results. Key: why each of the four things that can go wrong doesn't, citing the sentence for each.

  **Illustrations**, not bank items; their domains are used by no bank in this topic.
  - `trusts-partial`, Medium. The scenario:

    Nadia is hunting for an apartment near her new job.

    - Every morning a saving program saves the web page of each new listing on a rental site into the listings folder.
    - Then the assessing agent, following a skill file Nadia wrote, reads each saved page and writes a notes file for that listing on how well it fits her budget, her commute and her cat. It passes over any listing that already has a notes file.

    On Tuesday her laptop's battery ran out while the agent was writing the notes for the 31st of 52 new listings. Nadia hasn't opened any of the notes since.

    Question: "If Nadia runs the assessing agent again from the start, which of these happens?" Answer 4 (`half-written`): the 31st listing's notes file holds only part of its notes, and the rerun passes over it because the file exists. Not 1, because of the 31st. Not 2, because listings 1 to 30 have notes files and are passed over, and each listing has its own file. Not 3, because the agent does nothing outside the workflow. Not 5, because finished listings are passed over and Nadia hasn't opened any notes.
  - `already-safe`, variant `same-inputs`, Hard. The scenario:

    Jun watches an auction site for old film cameras.

    - Each night, for each new listing on the site, a saving program does two things: it saves a copy of the listing's web page in the listings folder, and it adds an entry for the listing to the cameras list. Nothing changes a saved page after it is saved.
    - Then the judging agent, following a skill file Jun wrote, reads each saved page and writes 'bid' or 'pass' into that listing's entry.
    - Then the details program pulls the price and the condition out of each saved page. It writes them into that listing's price and condition fields in the cameras list, overwriting whatever is there.

    On Sunday the details program stopped after 70 of 120 listings when the laptop restarted for an update. Since then Jun has looked over the first 70 entries and picked three to bid on.

    Answer 1 (`already-safe`): a program working from pages that never change writes the same price and condition it wrote before, so what Jun looked over stays as it was. Not 2, since it writes each listing's own fields rather than adding entries. Not 3, since it does nothing outside the workflow. Not 4, since the stop came between listings and the rerun rewrites every listing anyway. Not 5, since the result written is the same.
  - `acts-again`, Hard, in outline: Ruth's agent, following her skill file, reads a seed company's weekly newsletter and puts in an order for each seed packet that fits her garden plan. It passes over any packet on its finished list, which it saves when a run ends. The run stopped after ordering 9 of 15 packets, when she closed the laptop's lid and it went to sleep. Answer 3 (`duplicates`): the finished list was never saved, so the rerun orders the first 9 packets again.

  **How hard.** Medium when the deciding fact is plain: a step described with no test for done (which counts as plain), a test a partial result plainly passes, a person who has acted on results an agent will rewrite. Hard when it must be worked out: a test for done fooled because its record is written only at the end of a run; a `sound-skip` test where the stop came in the middle of writing; a `same-inputs` program whose results the person has already looked over; a `redoes-acted-on` scenario where the one acting on results is a later step rather than the person. The question wording is unchanged.

  **Spread across the bank.** A bank is eight scenarios, eight questions: `adds-again` and `acts-again` once each, so `duplicates` twice; `trusts-partial` twice; `redoes-acted-on` twice, once with a person acting on results and once with a later step; `already-safe` twice, once `sound-skip` and once `same-inputs`. Half of them Hard. At least one of the two `duplicates` scenarios has a test for done that the stopped run fools. The run stops in the middle of writing in both `trusts-partial` scenarios, at least one `redoes-acted-on` scenario and the `sound-skip` scenario. At least five scenarios are trackers with acquire, assess and extract steps, and at least two of the step's executors are programs. An option's position follows from its case (for `duplicates`, from its shape), so the answer's position needs no spreading. When the generator is run live, the tutor picks each next shape to carry a case the learner hasn't passed.

  **Domains** vary by scenario, none repeating within the bank: a concert-ticket resale watch, a secondhand furniture watch on a neighborhood swap site, a local events calendar built from venue newsletters, a youth soccer team's game schedule, a scholarship deadlines tracker, a family's receipts for taxes, a sneaker release tracker, a hiking-permit lottery tracker, a vintage-postcard watch. No domain from the other two question activities' lists.

  The rubric gives the `answer` (the 1-based choice) and, in a `tutor note`, the sentence that settles the sound option and, for each other option, the fact that rules it out; for a step with no test for done, that is the step's description and the absence of any test in it. Credit: full for the sound option; none for the others.
- **worked example:** shown only as help when asked for, which records the attempt as helped. The tutor works the illustration nearest the question's shape aloud (each illustration covers one shape; for a shape without one, the tutor works `trusts-partial` and says which fact would change the outcome), from the facts to the option: what the step does to each item, whether it passes over items done and whether the stopped run could fool its test, where the run stopped, and what has happened to the results since; then why each other option is ruled out. It never adds a fact the illustration leaves out. It may point to When a run stops partway in `tasks/read-failure-handling.md`. For an `adds-again` question, which has no illustration, the worked example is that section's bullet "Items done a second time"; for a `redoes-acted-on` question, its bullet "A different outcome replacing one already used". The tutor walks the learner through the bullet, adding no fact about the question's scenario.
- **doesn't show:** the learner picks among five options rather than saying what would go wrong, so a pass doesn't show they would name it unprompted; with five choices a guess is right a fifth of the time. Every scenario gives the step's test for done or describes the step with none, and states where the run stopped and who has acted on what, so a pass doesn't show the learner would find those facts in their own workflow, as the Nov 17 critique will ask. Each scenario has exactly one thing that goes wrong, so a pass shows nothing about a step where two would. No scenario has an agent rewriting results that nobody has acted on yet, which is safe for now without being idempotent, since option 1 claims idempotence. Nothing checks what to tell an agent to fix it.
- **offer as:** the quick check for rerunning: one step of a short personal workflow, a run that stopped partway through it, and you pick what running it again from the start would do. About 2 minutes a question.
- **check note:** On a `redoes-acted-on` question, a learner may argue for option 1 because the agent might give the same answer. Option 1 says the step is idempotent, and an agent that rewrites every result with no test for done is not, whatever it happens to answer this time.

### `a-pick-noticed-or-not`

- **serves:** `c-judge-detection`
- **supports:** attempt
- **checks:** `c-judge-detection`
- **artifact:** no external source. A described personal workflow, one of its steps and one mistake that step makes, told in an opening sentence, a bullet per step and a closing paragraph, with one three-choice question, from this activity's bank or written live per the generator below. About 2 minutes a question.
- **learner does:** reads the scenario, then picks whether the mistake would be noticed, and if not, which of the two reasons applies.
- **tutor role:** examiner
- **tutor does:** as `a-pick-rerun-outcome`'s, except that it labels a live run `a-pick-noticed-or-not/<shape>`.
- **done when:** the criterion met with no help, case by case. Each question gets one ruling: the sound option passes the case the question carries, and the others pass none. There is no half credit.
- **generator:** **A scenario** is one made-up personal workflow on one person's laptop, six to nine sentences in all, in the third person about that person, who is named. Every scenario in a bank names a different person, and none uses a name from this file's illustrations. It says what the workflow is for and what each step does, in plain words, and ends with what goes wrong in one step on one run.

  **Layout.** An opening sentence says who the person is and what they want. Then comes one bullet per step, in the order the steps run, each saying who does that step and what it does; a check, or a later step such as a shortlist program, gets its own bullet, and the person's routine goes in the bullet for what they read, or in its own. Then a paragraph says what happened: the mistake, and what the step did then. Sentences are short, one fact each. `tasks/a-pick-noticed-or-not/volunteer-openings.md` shows the shape. It follows `a-pick-rerun-outcome`'s rules "Kept things told apart" and "No test for done unless stated": a step does only what the scenario says it does.

  Every sentence about a step's work says who does it, a program, an agent following a skill file, or a person ("the program adds", "the agent passes over"), never "the step adds" or "a step goes through"; "the step" is only the name of the stage. At least half of a bank's scenarios are a small personal tracker with acquire, assess and extract steps, kept separate as in `a-pick-rerun-outcome` (the assessing agent judges; pulling fields out is the extract step's work), and at least one step is done by an agent following the person's skill file. In the bullets and the closing paragraph, never under labels, are these facts:
  - every check in the workflow (a program or an agent that looks over results) and exactly what it looks at ("a program confirms that every saved listing has an entry"; "a program flags any entry whose price field is empty"); a scenario may have none. Every check also says where it reports what it finds, and that place is always one the person sees before using the results ("puts any listing it flags at the top of the list Marta reads each evening"), so whether a check helps turns only on what it looks at. A check whose findings go somewhere outside the person's routine is never written: it would blur `unheard`, which is about the step, with a check;
  - every review the person does, what it covers and what it doesn't ("each evening Marta reads every entry marked 'worth a look', with the listing open beside it"; "she skims each entry's one-line summary");
  - where each step that can tell something went wrong says so: it stops and shows an error on the screen while the person is there to see it; it adds the item to a list of problems that the scenario says the person reads before using the results; it writes a note somewhere the person's routine doesn't take her ("writes a line to a problems file in the workflow's folder"), with nothing sending her there; it shows a warning in a window that closes when the run ends, on a run at a time the scenario's routine has the person away from the laptop; or it says nothing and goes on to the next item;
  - when runs happen, and when the person is at the laptop ("the runs start at 3 a.m."; "she opens the laptop each evening after work");
  - the mistake, in the closing paragraph, said as what happened, never judged: either one the step can tell happened (a page that won't open, a site that doesn't answer, a photo that won't load, a date in no form the program knows) and what the step does then; or one the step cannot tell happened, said as what came out ("took the posted date as the deadline", "rated the listing 'skip' though it meets every one of her needs", "filed a grocery receipt under 'medical'", "treated two different listings as one").

  **Unread by default.** A scenario never says a place goes unread ("a folder Dara never opens", "a note nobody opens", "a column she hid"). It says only what the person reads and when ("Each evening Dara reads the listings list ..."), and anywhere else is unread by default, as Would anyone notice? in `tasks/read-failure-handling.md` puts it: "Assume nobody looks anywhere without a reason: a place they check as part of their routine, or something that sends them there." A scenario keyed `detected` may have something send the person to a place (a line at the top of the list she reads saying problems were logged). A tutor note says why a place goes unread by citing the person's routine, never a sentence saying nobody opens it.

  **The question** reads: "Would this mistake be noticed?", then three numbered options, always in this order and these words:
  1. "Yes, it would be noticed."
  2. "No, a silent failure: the step can tell something went wrong, but no one would hear about it."
  3. "No, a silent failure: the result looks like a right one, and nothing in the workflow would catch it."

  `type: mcq`. The question carries the one case its answer exercises: answer 1 `detected`, answer 2 `unheard`, answer 3 `looks-right`.

  **Exactly one outcome.** A mistake the step can tell happened is keyed `detected` or `unheard`, never `looks-right`; one it cannot tell is keyed `detected` or `looks-right`, never `unheard`. A scenario keyed `detected` names the one thing that would bring the mistake to the person's attention (the stop seen on screen, the problems list read before the results are used or a line sending her to it, the check or review aimed at it). A scenario keyed `unheard` or `looks-right` states every check and review in the workflow, so the key can say why each misses this mistake. In a scenario keyed `unheard`, the failure leaves no visible trace in anything the person's routine reads: no empty field, missing value or odd mark in an entry she reads, which would make it `detected`. A skipped item that gets no entry at all is fine.

  **Shapes**, each with the case it carries. Between them every case is carried.
  - `heard` (`detected`): four variants, which the key names. `stops-in-view`: the step stops with an error on screen, and the scenario says the person starts each run herself and waits for it to finish. `listed`: the step puts the item on a list of problems the person reads before using the results, or writes it to a problems file and something sends her there (a line at the top of the list she reads saying problems were logged). `aimed-check`: a check looks at exactly what went wrong, and reports where the person sees it before using the results (a program that compares the number of entries the assessing agent made with the number of saved listings, when that agent, which works from the saved listings and adds one entry per listing, passed over one; a program that compares each entry's price with the price on the saved page, when the step took the wrong figure). A count always counts what the skipping step itself produces, against something that step didn't make: the saved items, or the number the source itself lists. `aimed-review`: the person's review covers what went wrong (she reads every listing marked 'worth a look' in full, and the mistake is a listing wrongly marked 'worth a look').
  - `unheard` (`unheard`): three variants. In all three the failure leaves no visible trace in what the person reads, as "Exactly one outcome" says: an extracting program that can't read a date and leaves the entry's date empty, when the person reads that entry, is `detected`, not `unheard`. `goes-on-quietly`: the step passes over the item and says nothing, and any check in the workflow looks only at entries that exist (that every entry has a price, say), so the missing one gives it nothing to flag. The scenario always says that the kept thing gets an entry only for an item that was saved or processed ("the agent adds an entry for each saved menu"), so it plainly holds no entry for the skipped item. `unopened-note`: the step writes what went wrong to a place the person's routine, as the scenario gives it, doesn't take her, and nothing sends her there; the scenario never says the place goes unread. `unwatched-window`: the step shows a warning in a window that closes when the run ends, on a run at a time the scenario gives, when its routine has the person away from the laptop (the runs start at 3 a.m., and she opens the laptop each evening). In none of them does the run stop.
  - `looks-right` (`looks-right`): three variants. `no-check`: nothing in the workflow looks over this step's results. `check-elsewhere`: a check exists and looks at something other than the mistake (that every entry has a deadline, when the deadline is the wrong date; that the entries match the saved listings in number, when one listing got the wrong rating). `review-elsewhere`: the person reviews, but not this (she checks each entry's date against the listing, and the mistake is in its price; she reads the summaries, and the mistake is in a figure the summary leaves out).

  **Not a copy of an illustration.** A bank scenario differs from the illustrations in what its check looks at and what its review covers. No bank scenario has a check that confirms every item has a mark beside a judging agent's marks, as the Marta pair does, or a person who reads only the items with one mark, as Marta does; and a `goes-on-quietly` scenario's check looks at something other than an empty price range, as Eli's does.

  **Illustrations**, not bank items; their domains are used by no bank in this topic.
  - `review-elsewhere`, Medium. The scenario:

    Marta is looking for a used cargo bike.

    - Every two hours, for each new listing on two classified sites, a saving program does two things: it saves a copy of the listing's web page in the bikes folder, and it adds an entry for the listing to the bikes list.
    - Then the judging agent, following a skill file Marta wrote, reads each saved page and marks the listing's entry 'worth a look' or 'skip' against her budget and the features she needs.
    - Then a checking program confirms that every entry in the bikes list has a mark. It puts any entry without one at the top of the list.
    - Each evening Marta reads the saved page of every listing marked 'worth a look' in full, with its photos.

    On Thursday the agent marked 'skip' a bike that is under her budget and has every feature she needs.

    Answer 3 (`looks-right`): a 'skip' mark is a plausible result; the checking program looks only at whether each entry has a mark, and Marta reads only the 'worth a look' ones. Not 2, since the agent has no way to tell its mark is wrong. Not 1, since neither the check nor the review looks at listings marked 'skip'.
  - `aimed-review`, Medium: the same workflow, with the closing paragraph instead "On Thursday the agent marked 'worth a look' a bike that costs twice her budget." Answer 1 (`detected`): Marta reads every 'worth a look' listing in full, so she will see the price. This pair shows why a review counts only for what it looks at; a bank uses such a pair in two domains, never one.
  - `goes-on-quietly`, Hard, in outline: Eli's program saves the menus of restaurants he wants to try from a city food site each week, and an agent adds an entry to his list for each saved menu, with its price range and opening days; a restaurant whose menu wasn't saved gets no entry. A program then flags any entry with an empty price range, at the top of the list Eli reads before choosing where to eat. One week the site doesn't answer for one restaurant, and the saving program goes on to the next without a word. Answer 2 (`unheard`): the program could tell the site didn't answer, but said nothing, and the flagging program looks only at entries that exist, so a restaurant with no entry gives it nothing to flag.

  **How hard.** Medium when one fact settles it and no check or review is near the mistake. Hard when a check or review is near it and the learner must see what it looks at (`aimed-check`, `check-elsewhere`, `review-elsewhere` with a review that covers the same step, `goes-on-quietly` with a check present), or when a file of problems is read (`listed`). The question wording is unchanged.

  **Spread across the bank.** A bank is seven scenarios, seven questions: `heard` twice, once `listed` and once `aimed-check` or `aimed-review`; `unheard` twice, once `goes-on-quietly` with a check present and once `unopened-note` or `unwatched-window`; `looks-right` three times, once in each variant. At least four scenarios have a check or a review, and in at least three of them it looks at something other than the mistake. At least one pair of scenarios has the same kind of review or check and is keyed differently, as the Marta pair is. Half of them Hard. An option's position follows from its case. When the generator is run live, the tutor picks each next shape to carry a case the learner hasn't passed.

  **Domains** are this activity's own, none repeating within the bank: a campsite availability watch, a used-textbook price tracker, a volunteer opportunities tracker from community newsletters, a pet adoption listings watch, a farmers market vendor list, a running-race sign-up tracker, a houseplant cuttings swap board, a neighborhood lost-and-found tracker. No domain from the other two question activities' lists.

  The rubric gives the `answer` (the 1-based choice) and, in a `tutor note`, the sentence that settles the sound option, whether the step could tell something went wrong, where it says so and whether the person's routine (what she reads and when) or something sending her there takes her to that place, and every check and review in the scenario with what it looks at and why it does or doesn't reach this mistake. Credit: full for the sound option; none for the others.
- **worked example:** shown only as help when asked for, which records the attempt as helped. The tutor works the illustration nearest the question's shape aloud: whether the step could tell something went wrong, where it says so and whether the person's routine takes her there, then each check and review and what it looks at, and whether any of them reaches this mistake. It never adds a fact the illustration leaves out. It may point to Would anyone notice? in `tasks/read-failure-handling.md`.
- **doesn't show:** the learner picks among three options rather than saying why a mistake would go unnoticed, so a pass doesn't show they would name the reason unprompted; with three choices a guess is right a third of the time. Every scenario states every check and review and what each looks at, and what the person reads and when, so a pass doesn't show the learner would find these out about their own workflow, or think of a mistake a step could make; the mistake is always given. Every check reports where the person sees it, so a pass shows nothing about a check whose own findings go unread. Nothing checks what to add so that a step fails loudly.
- **offer as:** the quick check for noticing: a short personal workflow and one mistake in one of its steps, and you pick whether it would be noticed, and if not, why. About 2 minutes a question.
- **check note:** The reading's Would anyone notice? section says a check that every row of the grades sheet has a score would catch a skipped submission. That holds only because the TA's sheet already has a row for each submission. In this activity's scenarios, a check over the entries catches a skipped item only if the scenario says an entry exists for it. If a learner cites the reading on a `goes-on-quietly` question, say this.

### `a-critique-a-workflow`

- **serves:** `c-judge-rerunnable`, `c-judge-detection`
- **supports:** attempt
- **checks:** `c-judge-rerunnable`, `c-judge-detection`
- **artifact:** no external source. One whole personal tracker workflow, three or four steps told in a short opening paragraph and a bullet for each step, then six questions about it, asked one at a time: three about rerunning a different step after a stopped run, then three about a mistake in one step. From this activity's bank or written live per the generator below. About 4 minutes to read the scenario and answer the first question, and under 2 for each after, so about 12 for a scenario.
- **learner does:** reads the workflow once, then answers its questions one at a time, each a pick from the same options as the quick checks: for a stopped run, what running that step again from the start would do; for a mistake, whether it would be noticed, and if not, why. This is the shape of the Nov 17 class's critique, on a workflow that isn't theirs.
- **tutor role:** examiner
- **tutor does:** sets each question as served, in file order, without rewording it or hinting; never names the cases, which fact matters, or which option the key expects, and never says what is wrong with an option before the learner has chosen. Gives help whenever it is asked for, and records the attempt as helped. When the generator is run live, writes the key, goal and case into the record before showing each question. Labels the attempt with the question's path, or `a-critique-a-workflow/<domain>/<question>` when run live, and records the case with `--cases`. Once a scenario's remaining questions would carry only cases the learner has already passed, skips them, as study skips questions no longer needed. Rules from the rubric's `answer`.
- **done when:** the criterion met with no help, case by case, for each goal separately: a question's sound option passes the one case it carries, of the one goal it names.
- **generator:** **A scenario** is one made-up personal tracker on one person's laptop, with acquire, assess and extract steps and optionally a fourth step that uses their results (a weekly shortlist, a post for each pick to a board other people read, an order). It is ten to fourteen sentences in all, in the third person about the person, who is named.

  **Layout.** A short opening paragraph says who the person is, what they want and when the tracker runs. Then comes one bullet per step, in the order the steps run, each saying who does that step and what it does; a check, or a later step such as a shortlist program, gets its own bullet, and the person's routine goes in the bullet for what they read, or in its own. The setup has no closing paragraph: each stop or mistake, and what has happened since, comes in its question's own supposition. Sentences are short, one fact each. `tasks/a-critique-a-workflow/cheap-flights.md` shows the shape. It follows `a-pick-rerun-outcome`'s rules "Kept things told apart" and "No test for done unless stated": a step does only what the scenario says it does.

  Every scenario in a bank names a different person, and none uses a name from this file's illustrations. Each step has a short handle, and every sentence about a step's work says who does it ("the program adds", "the agent passes over"), never "the step adds"; "the step" is only the name of the stage. The acquire, assess and extract steps are kept separate as in `a-pick-rerun-outcome`: the assessing agent judges each item, and pulling fields out is the extract step's work. The assess step, at least, is done by an agent following a skill file the person wrote. For each step the bullets state, in plain words and never under labels, the facts `a-pick-rerun-outcome` and `a-pick-noticed-or-not` require: who does it, what it does with each item, any test it uses to pass over items already done, what it writes and where, where it says so when it can tell something went wrong, and when runs happen; and for the whole workflow, every check and every review the person does, and exactly what each looks at. The scenario says nothing about any stop or mistake; those come in the questions. It follows both quick checks' rules on words, and `a-pick-noticed-or-not`'s unread-by-default rule: it says what the person reads and when, never that a place goes unread, and a tutor note cites that routine.

  **Questions**, each its own `###` question under the scenario's shared setup, never parts of a compound, in this order:
  - three rerun questions, each about a different step, in the order the steps run. Each begins with one or two sentences supposing a stop and what has happened since ("Suppose the assessing agent stops while it is writing the notes for the 12th of 30 listings, and nobody has opened any notes since."), then reads "If <person> runs <step> again from the start, which of these happens?" with `a-pick-rerun-outcome`'s five options, in its order and words. `type: mcq`; goal `c-judge-rerunnable`; it carries the case of its answer.
  - three detection questions, each about a mistake in one step. Each begins with one sentence saying what goes wrong, said as `a-pick-noticed-or-not` says a mistake ("Suppose one week the collecting program can't open one site's page."), then reads "Would this mistake be noticed?" with that activity's three options, in its order and words. `type: mcq`; goal `c-judge-detection`; it carries the case of its answer.

  Every fact a question's answer needs is either in the shared setup or in that question's own supposition. A supposition never states a fact about the workflow that bears on another question (it adds only where a run stopped, what has happened to results since, or what went wrong), so no question gives away another's answer. Each question's step and supposition must give exactly one sound option, under the "Exactly one outcome" rules of the quick check it borrows from.

  **Cases.** A scenario's three rerun questions carry three different cases of `c-judge-rerunnable`, and its three detection questions carry all three cases of `c-judge-detection`, one each. The workflow is written so this falls out of the steps as they are: for example, a collecting program that adds an entry for each new item and is described with no test for done (`duplicates`), an assessing agent that passes over any item that already has a notes file (`half-written`, when it stops while writing), and an extracting program that works from saved pages that never change (`already-safe`, even after the person has looked over its results). For detection: a check that counts the assessing agent's own output (its notes files, or the entries carrying its rating) against the number of saved items, and puts any shortfall where the person sees it before using the results (`detected`, for a saved item the assessing agent passed over because it couldn't open it: that item has no notes file or rating, so the counts differ), a problems file the person's routine never takes her to, with nothing sending her there and no visible trace of the failure in anything she reads, as `a-pick-noticed-or-not`'s "Exactly one outcome" rule says (`unheard`), and a review of only the items rated 'worth a look', with the mistake an item wrongly rated 'skip' (`looks-right`). As in `a-pick-noticed-or-not`, every check reports where the person sees it, and a count check always counts what the skipping step itself produces, against something that step didn't make. At least one detection question per scenario is keyed `unheard` or `looks-right` despite a check or review in the workflow, which looks at something other than its mistake.

  **How hard.** Each question is Medium or Hard by the rules of the quick check it borrows from; each scenario has at least two Hard questions. Bullets of facts about several steps, only some of which bear on a given question, make every scenario harder than a quick-check one.

  **Spread across the bank.** A bank is three scenarios, eighteen questions. Across them each case of `c-judge-rerunnable` is carried at least twice, and each case of `c-judge-detection` three times, once per scenario. An option's position follows from its case.

  **Domains** are this activity's own, none repeating within the bank: a cheap-flights tracker built from deal newsletters, a research article alert tracker for a class project, a sublet listings tracker for a summer away, a local art fair vendor tracker. No domain from the other two question activities' lists.

  The rubric gives each question's `goal`, `cases`, and `answer` (the 1-based choice), and in a `tutor note`, the sentences that settle the sound option and the fact that rules out each other option, as the quick checks' rubrics do; for a step with no test for done, that is the step's description and the absence of any test in it. Credit: full for the sound option; none for the others.
- **worked example:** shown only as help when asked for, which records the attempt as helped. The tutor works one question of the same kind aloud on the matching quick check's illustration, as that activity's worked example does, then points the learner back to their own question's step and the facts about it in the setup. It may point to When a run stops partway and Would anyone notice? in `tasks/read-failure-handling.md`.
- **doesn't show:** as both quick checks', and more: the questions supply each stop and each mistake, so a pass doesn't show the learner would think of which stops and mistakes to worry about in a workflow, which the Nov 17 critique asks of them; and every check and review is named in the setup. A scenario carries only three of the four rerun cases, so meeting `c-judge-rerunnable` through this activity takes a second scenario or one quick-check question.
- **offer as:** the critique check, closest to the Nov 17 class: one whole tracker workflow and six questions about it, three on rerunning a step after a stop and three on whether a mistake would be noticed, each picked from the same options as the quick checks. About 12 minutes. Harder than the quick checks, since the facts for each question sit among facts about other steps.
