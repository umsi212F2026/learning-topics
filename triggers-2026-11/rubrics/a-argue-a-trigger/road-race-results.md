Shape `person-or-schedule`, variant `person`, case `not-event`, Medium. Runs are rare and
irregular, and the race director is at the finish line anyway, uploading the times and reading the
page over.

Accepted: the race director starting the run herself once she has uploaded the finish times. A
design where her upload starts the run is ruled as the person starting it, and accepted; so is a
design that checks every few minutes for uploaded times and starts the run once they are there.

The deciding facts, and the sentences carrying them:

- The irregular need: "We put on four or five races a year, and the dates change from year to
  year, depending on when the parks are free." and "On race day, the last runner can finish
  anywhere from noon to two in the afternoon, depending on the course and the weather."
- The person's presence, supplying the input and looking over the result: "Once the last runner
  is in, I upload the finish times from the timing clock, and the agent builds the page from
  them." and "I read the page over as soon as it is built, there at the finish line, before it
  goes on the department's website."

Why the other kinds fit worse:

- A schedule at a regular time (weekly, monthly): there are only four or five races a year on
  dates that change, so nearly every run would come when there is no race and nothing to build.
- A schedule set for a fixed time on race day: the last runner can finish anywhere from noon to
  two, so a run at 12:30 could come before the times are uploaded, and one at 2:30 would leave her
  waiting at the finish line after an early finish; the dates change too, so it would have to be
  set again for every race.
- An event other than her own upload: nothing happens on its own that means the race is over; the
  times are ready only when she uploads them, which is the person starting it.

Never accepted, by name: a regular weekly or monthly schedule, which mostly runs with no race to
report; a fixed race-day time, which can fire before the last runner is in.

The scenario does not say how reliably she remembers, or when the laptop is on. An answer resting
on either brings in a fact from outside the scenario.

Irrelevant detail: "Our biggest race is a 10K along the river."

Generic reasons that fail: "a person can make sure it's done right", "a schedule is automatic",
"it's simpler to do it by hand".

True facts with no bearing on the choice, which also fail: the page goes on the department's
website; the timing clock records the finish times.

Examples of each kind of tied reason, for the person starting it:

- What makes it good here: "there are only four or five races a year on dates that change, and
  she is right there uploading the times, so she can start it when she does."
- What would go wrong here without it: "a run set for 12:30 on race day could come before the last
  runner finishes, so the page would be built from incomplete times or none."
- What would make a specific other kind worse: "a weekly schedule would run about fifty times a
  year for four or five races, nearly always with nothing to build."
